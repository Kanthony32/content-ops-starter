Horns Slip

Enter it. Get it back. Slip the big behind the defense. 🏀

By Keil Moore · October 30, 2026

▶ Watch the Horns Slip breakdown on Instagram

Film and diagram: @basketballscience8642 on Instagram

Horns is the most common half-court alignment you’ll see this season: a guard at the top, two bigs at the elbows, two shooters in the corners. Everybody knows the look. That’s the point. The defense knows what’s coming, so the counter is where you score.

The Slip is one of the cleanest counters in the series. It takes two things the defense is already worried about, a handoff and a pin-in, and uses them to hide a big rolling to the rim.

📖 What it is

The point guard enters the ball to the elbow, follows the pass and gets it right back on a handoff. While the defense is locked on that exchange, the opposite big steps down like he’s setting a pin-in for the corner, then slips to the rim behind the defense. The guard hits him for a layup.

Elbow entry → handoff back to 1 → fake pin-in → slip → layup

If you read our How to Watch the Game piece, you already know the first half of this. Pass, follow, handoff: that’s Get. Horns Slip is Get out of a Horns alignment with a slip hidden behind it.

📍 The setup

1Top of the key, middle third of the floor, eyes over both elbows. 4 & 5At the elbows. 4 is the entry side; 5 is the slip man. 2 & 3Corners, feet behind the line. They hold there. No early lift.

🔢 The action, step by step

  1. Entry. 1 passes to 4 at the elbow. That’s the trigger.
  2. Follow. 1 chases the pass. Not a stroll; he’s coming to get the ball back.
  3. Handoff. 4 hands it back to 1, and 1 turns the corner toward the middle of the floor.
  4. The fake. At the same time, 5 steps down toward 2’s defender like he’s setting a pin-in. Sell it.
  5. The slip. As 1 comes off the handoff, 5 opens to the ball and slips to the rim, behind the defenders who are looking at the handoff and the pin-in.
  6. The finish. 1 hits 5 on the move. Layup. 2 and 3 never left the corners, so there’s nobody in the paint to rescue it.

📺 What to look for on TV

Don’t follow the handoff. Watch the big at the opposite elbow.

When the guard gives up the ball at the elbow and runs after it, that’s your cue. Find the other big. Does he walk toward the corner like he’s going to screen? Then watch his defender. If that defender turns his head to find the pin-in, or steps up to help on the guard coming off the handoff, the slip is open before the screen ever happens.

Then look at the corners. If the corner defenders are glued to their shooters, nobody is home at the rim.

🧠 Why it works

  • It occupies the right defenders. The handoff pulls attention toward the ball. The pin-in fake pulls the slip man’s defender toward the corner. That’s two defenders looking the wrong way.
  • The corners hold the help. With 2 and 3 spotted up deep, the low defenders have to choose: tag the slip or stay home on a shooter.
  • It beats a defense that sits back. If the big’s defender is playing back to protect the paint, he’s sinking toward the action, not trailing the slip to the rim.
  • It’s a counter, not a play call. The defense has seen Horns entries all game. The slip punishes them for guarding the pattern instead of the player.

🛡️ How to guard it

Talk before the entry, not after the slip.

The defender on 5 has the hardest job. He can’t chase the pin-in on faith. Stay between your man and the rim until he actually screens. If he’s slipping, you have to be there first.

The corner defenders need a plan, too. Who tags the slip? Who stays home? If the answer is “we’ll figure it out,” you’re giving up the layup or the corner three, and the offense gets to pick.

And on the handoff, don’t send two at the ball. Every extra defender on the exchange is one fewer defender at the rim.

🏀 How to run it

  • Timing is everything. 5 slips as 1 turns the corner off the handoff. Too early and the defense sees it. Too late and 1 has already picked up his dribble.
  • Sell the pin-in. 5 has to look like a screener for a full step. If he never sells it, his defender never leaves.
  • 4 sells the handoff. A lazy handoff lets the defense skip it and sit on the slip.
  • 1 reads the rim, not the ball. Eyes on 5’s defender. If he turns his hips toward the corner, the pass is there.
  • Corners hold. 2 and 3 don’t lift until the ball is in the paint. Lifting early brings their defenders with them, right into the slip lane.

🔀 Counters off the same look

  • If the low man tags the slip: 1 skips it to the corner he left. That’s the shot the spacing was built for.
  • If 1’s defender jumps the handoff: 4 keeps it and turns the corner himself. The fake handoff is a weapon too.
  • If 5’s defender stays home: 5 actually sets the pin-in, and 2 comes off it for a catch-and-shoot.

Same alignment, three answers. That’s what a series is.
