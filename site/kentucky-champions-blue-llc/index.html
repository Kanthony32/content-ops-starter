Kentucky Just Turned Its Athletic Department Into a Business

Champions Blue LLC is built to borrow, raise money and make deals the way a company does. Every athletic director in the country is watching the film.

By Keil Moore · November 11, 2026

Disclosure: HoopThink is affiliated with The Westview Group, which also does athlete-representation and NIL-related work.

Kentucky did not rebrand its athletic department. It moved it into a company.

On April 25, 2025, the University of Kentucky Board of Trustees approved Champions Blue, LLC, a holding company that takes over the department, with the move set to play out over the following year. Kentucky was widely described as the first school to convert an athletic department this way. The reason is not complicated. Revenue sharing arrived, and a department that ran close to break-even suddenly had a new line item.

The film is worth studying whether you run a program or just follow one.

What Kentucky Actually Did

This is not a startup bolted onto the side of campus. Kentucky built Champions Blue on a model it already runs.

  • The template is Beyond Blue Corporation. It is the university’s existing holding company, and Champions Blue is a subsidiary of it. UK HealthCare used that structure to move quickly on its purchases of King’s Daughters Medical Center in Ashland and St. Claire HealthCare in Morehead.
  • The university keeps control. A Board of Governors runs the entity: seven voting members, four from senior university leadership (President Eli Capilouto, who chairs, among them) and three outside experts from professional sports, real estate, and events and facilities.
  • The athletic director does not run the board. Mitch Barnhart sits as a non-voting advisor, alongside JMI Sports president Paul Archey.
  • Same legal footing. UK’s board documents describe Champions Blue as an alter ego of the university with the same immunity under Kentucky law.

The Numbers

The margin tells the story. Before revenue sharing, Kentucky athletics operated with very little cushion.

The loans are repaid with interest. In plain terms, the university is funding the build-out and the deficit gap from its own reserves while Champions Blue works to generate new revenue.

Why an LLC Changes the Game

Here is the scouting report on what the structure unlocks, based on how the university and outside analysts describe it.

  • Debt capital. A dedicated entity and board can use loans and credit lines with a faster approval process than a department sitting inside a university.
  • Direct fundraising. The department can raise money itself rather than routing everything through a third-party NIL collective.
  • Real estate and partnerships. UK points to public-private partnerships, real estate, and expanded premium seating and fan experience.
  • Outside investment. Analysts see a path to external capital. Kentucky has not announced anything like that. Others have.

One early signal on the commercial side: the Champions Blue Board of Governors endorsed an amended multimedia rights agreement with JMI Sports at its very first meeting.

Who Is Next

The early followers show the model traveling.

  • Utah created its own LLC to house athletics a few months after Kentucky, and its version brought a private equity investor into the entity, according to a 2026 article in the SMU Law Review.
  • Mississippi State athletic director Zac Selmon said his school was considering its own plan after reading about Champions Blue.
  • The industry read: one sports-business analyst told Boardroom flatly, “More schools will follow.”

Two programs, two different appetites for risk, one shared goal: more ways to raise money before the revenue-sharing bill comes due.

What to Watch

The structure is clear. The results are not. Here is what separates a blueprint from a cautionary tale.

  • Can it out-earn the debt? The $141M in internal loans has to be repaid with interest. Which of the new revenue streams actually shows up?
  • Tax exposure. Analysts note that once a structure like this turns a profit, it could face separate taxation. Reports also differ on how to characterize the entity, so the tax picture is worth confirming with the university.
  • Transparency. The board calls itself a public entity, but holding-company finances can be harder to read than a department budget. How much will fans and lawmakers actually see?
  • Title IX and equity. Outside investment raises the stakes for non-revenue sports and women’s programs. Who protects them when the model is built around return?

Is the holding-company model the new standard for the biggest programs, or a Kentucky play built on a health-care template?
