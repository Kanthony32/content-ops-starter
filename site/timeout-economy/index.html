Think Piece Coaching and game management

The Timeout Economy

Are women’s basketball coaches saving timeouts at the expense of winning? A timeout sitting on the clipboard has no competitive value. Its value comes from what the coach does with it.

By Keil Moore · November 5, 2026

Here’s the position up front: coaches may be holding timeouts for a late-game possession at the cost of stopping runs early in the game.

That’s a coaching philosophy problem before it’s a rules problem. It’s also a game-management problem. The bigger question isn’t simply whether coaches should call more timeouts. It’s whether they understand the opportunity cost of saving one.

I want to make the argument with evidence, not just instinct. Here’s what the research says, where it’s thin, and what we should measure next.

The Scenario

Take a team with a 12-point lead at halftime. The opponent comes out of the break and goes on a 7-0 run. This is a scenario, not a specific game, and the numbers are illustrative.

In the NBA, the default is an immediate timeout. In women’s college basketball, the default seems to be waiting for the media timeout at the 5-minute mark.

Part of that wait is built into the format. Under the NCAA’s media timeout guidelines (the 2021-23 edition), each quarter gets one electronic-media timeout at the first dead ball at or below the 5-minute mark. A run that starts at the top of the third quarter can go five minutes before the league’s own stoppage arrives.

A team timeout called before that mark works differently. It becomes that quarter’s media timeout. The stop comes sooner, but the scheduled one is used up. That’s the trade.

I think coaches are doing it wrong. A 7-0 run to open a half is the moment to stop the game, not the moment to wait for the schedule to do it.

1. What the Research Tells Us

There’s real research on timeout value. Some of it is strong. Some of it is narrower than the headlines suggest. Here’s how I’d read it.

NCAA Division I men’s basketball (2019 study). The authors examined timeouts in Division I men’s games. They found teams performed roughly 1.5 to 2.2 points better in the five minutes after a called timeout than their pre-timeout expectation. The headline number is an expectation-adjusted gap, not a raw before-and-after comparison, and the raw figures don’t fully line up with it. The authors also use causal language even though the data are observational. Treat it as a strong association, not a proven value.

EuroLeague (2021-22 through 2023-24 seasons). The average point differential improved from a 3.74-point deficit in the minute before a timeout to a 1.20-point deficit in the minute after. The authors’ framing is the one I’d use: timeouts limit deterioration more than they reverse it.

Spanish ACB League (2009-10 season). Sixty games and 436 timeout observations. The calling team scored 1.59 more points over the next three possessions, 2.10 over five, and 2.29 over ten. The abstract doesn’t report significance levels, so I’d present these as directional.

NBA (2009-2012, more than 3,000 games). This one cuts against the easy story. Saavedra, Mukherjee and Bagrow found that a coach’s timeout effectiveness was negatively associated with experience. In their words, “timeout factor is negatively associated with coaching experience.” Timeouts are not a fixed asset that good coaches always turn into points. How they’re used matters.

What this adds up to: Timeouts have measurable short-term value. The effect is usually a stabilizer, not a reversal. And the value depends on the decision.

That’s the gap. None of these studies covers NCAA women’s basketball timeouts directly.

2. Do Timeouts Actually Stop Runs?

This is where the research gets most interesting, because it doesn’t rely on coaches’ decisions.

A 2023 study in the Journal of Sports Analytics examined NBA television timeouts. These are mandated by the league, so they’re not chosen in response to momentum. The authors matched runs of six or more unanswered points that were interrupted by a TV timeout with similar runs that weren’t.

The finding: teams with momentum scored about 0.56 fewer points over the next three minutes than comparable teams without the interruption. The authors reported an 11.2 percent decline in points scored by the team on the run.

Two caveats matter here. First, the effect blends the stoppage with any adjustments a team makes during it, and that split needs checking before anyone cites it. Second, the longer-run results are more sensitive. The 15-point run estimate rests on only 48 observations, and the authors warn against overreading it. The authors also found no significant relationship between the size of the run and the effect, so I wouldn’t claim “bigger runs, bigger stop.”

The simplest point is the most useful one: the interruption itself has value. A timeout is a stoppage that gives a coach a chance to reset.

So why are coaches voluntarily allowing an opponent to keep playing through a run when they have an instrument designed to interrupt it?

3. When Should a Coach Call a Timeout?

I’d organize the coaching decision around five situations. These are coaching categories, not statistical laws.

  • Opponent scoring run: Interrupt rhythm and prevent additional damage.
  • Offensive stagnation: Reset organization and establish an entry.
  • Defensive breakdown: Change coverage, matchups or personnel.
  • Loss of composure: Settle players and eliminate rushed decisions.
  • Critical game-management situation: Advance the ball or establish a late-game possession.

The first four can happen at virtually any point in a game. The fifth is situational, and it’s the one that tempts coaches to hold timeouts back.

That distinction is fundamental. Most of the timeouts that matter in a game happen before the final minute.

4. Triggers to Investigate

These are coaching guidelines to test, not established thresholds. I’d want the data before anyone calls them rules.

  • The 7-0 run. Seven unanswered points, especially to open a half, is enough to stop the game. Ask what’s driving it: coverage breakdowns, live-ball turnovers, transition defense, low-quality offensive possessions, or a personnel mismatch.
  • The 10-2 run. That’s a real change in game conditions. Is the game plan still working?
  • Three consecutive defensive breakdowns. The scoreboard doesn’t always show the problem. Three missed rotations in a row can justify a timeout even if the opponent has scored only four points.
  • The opponent changed the game. A switch from man-to-man to a trapping zone changes the offensive environment. A timeout can set the counter before the next possession.
  • The team is losing control. This is probably the most important trigger. Sometimes the timeout isn’t about one play. It’s about keeping a team from losing its composure, its identity and its discipline.

A good coach can tell a bad possession from a team beginning to unravel. The timeout is the tool for the second one.

5. The Economics of Saving One

This is where I’d challenge the prevailing philosophy.

Suppose a team leads by 12 at halftime. The opponent opens the second half on a 7-0 run. The coach has three timeouts left.

Approach A: Wait. Let the run play until the 5-minute stop. Hope the team settles. Keep the timeouts for a possible late-game possession.

Approach B: Stop it now. Stop the run. Make a defensive adjustment. Get the ball to the right players. Reset the mental approach. Keep the remaining timeouts for later.

I favor Approach B, for two reasons.

First, a late-game possession is an opportunity, not a guarantee. It doesn’t ensure a basket, a better shot or a successful possession. And the team has to reach a late-game situation where it’s needed at all.

Second, a timeout early in the third quarter can keep a 12-point lead from turning into a game the team has to chase. That’s a tangible, immediate cost of waiting.

My working hypothesis: the expected value of a timeout used to prevent a substantial collapse can exceed its expected value as a late-game mechanism. We need data to quantify that. We shouldn’t just assert it.

6. The Study We Should Run

I’d go beyond the existing research and build our own dataset of NCAA Division I women’s games, ideally across several seasons, including any seasons with different timeout allotments.

The variables that matter:

  • Timeout timing: quarter and game clock.
  • Score differential when the timeout is called.
  • Run length: the opponent’s consecutive scoring.
  • Time from the start of a run to the first stoppage, including scheduled media timeouts.
  • Timeouts remaining.
  • Post-timeout performance over the next three, five and ten possessions.
  • Whether defensive coverage changed.
  • Whether offensive execution improved.
  • Final result and win probability.

The questions the data must answer:

  • How often do teams allow 8-, 10- and 12-point runs without calling a timeout?
  • What happens to scoring efficiency over the next five possessions when a timeout is called versus when it isn’t?
  • How often do teams finish games with timeouts unused?
  • How often does a team actually use a timeout for a late-game possession?
  • Do teams that save timeouts for the final minute outperform teams that spend them earlier?

One methodological problem matters more than the rest. You can’t compare scoring before and after a timeout and declare it successful. Coaches call timeouts when their teams are already struggling. That’s selection bias. The comparison has to be between similar game situations, adjusting for score, time remaining, opponent strength, home and away, possession, the run entering the timeout, and timeouts available.

We should also measure win probability added, not just points. A timeout that stops a 6-0 run and produces a clean offensive possession may matter more than one that produces an isolated basket.

7. My Central Argument

I’m not arguing that coaches should call a timeout every time an opponent scores three straight baskets. That would be simplistic.

In my experience, coaches who had five timeouts used them to stop runs and reorganize more often. That’s the comparison I’d test.

I’m arguing that timeout management has become too focused on preserving inventory. Timeouts are coaching resources. Their value is highest when they’re used to address a specific problem.

The smaller timeout allotment may have encouraged coaches to think about preservation instead of deployment. That’s the distinction worth examining.

A timeout sitting unused on the coach’s clipboard has no competitive value. Its value comes from what the coach accomplishes by using it.

8. The Question I’d Chase Hardest

How many games end with a coach holding one or more timeouts?

My sense from watching games is that teams are finishing with timeouts still on the bench. That’s the claim to test, and it may be the most revealing number in this whole discussion. If coaches routinely finish games with timeouts unused while also allowing double-digit runs earlier, that doesn’t prove the decisions were wrong. But it gives us a way to ask whether timeout conservation has become a habit rather than a choice.

I’d separate two things: timeouts left at the end of regulation, and timeouts that were available during critical stretches but never used. They’re different problems.

Where This Leaves Us

I’m not writing an opinion piece dressed up with statistics. The research is good enough to say timeouts can improve short-term performance. It isn’t yet enough to assign a points value to a timeout in NCAA women’s basketball, or to prove that saving one for a late-game possession is a losing strategy.

That gap is the work.

So here’s my question for coaches, analysts and anyone who has sat through a run and wondered why the timeout didn’t come: When a run goes unanswered and the only thing stopping the bleeding is the scheduled five-minute media timeout, are we looking at a game plan or simply following a schedule? Are you controlling the ship, or are you just letting the wind dictate where it goes?
