The Best On-Ball Defenders Solve the Ball Screen the Same Way: Shrink the Space, Then Swing Step

Mike Jagacki’s latest breakdown puts Jrue Holiday, Dyson Daniels, Tony Allen and Dwyane Wade on the same clock. They come from different eras, with different bodies, on different teams. The footwork is identical.

By Keil Moore · November 23, 2026

That consistency is the signal.

Here is the post that started it:

Mike Jagacki’s ball-screen footwork breakdown of Jrue Holiday, Dyson Daniels, Tony Allen and Dwyane Wade.

— Mike Jagacki (@mike_jagacki) View the post on X

What the Sequence Does

The on-ball defender’s first job in a ball screen is to take away the space the handler wants. Fighting over and going under are responses to a problem the defender has already lost.

  • Shrink space. The defender closes the gap before the screener arrives. That shortens the pocket pass, cuts the driving angle and squeezes the handler into a tighter window.
  • Swing step. Once the screen is set, the trail foot swings through in a short, low pivot that clears the screener’s body. The defender keeps contact and balance, chest square and hips ready to recover. It is not a full crossover or a drop step.

The four clips show the same timing and intent. Only the expression changes. Allen leans on physicality and early contact. Wade uses craft and hip control. Holiday wins with anticipation and balance. Daniels recovers with length and speed.

Why It Matters Now

Modern offenses are built to create and use space in the ball-screen game. A defender who can’t consistently shrink that space gets attacked in the pocket, forced into switches the scheme doesn’t want, or left recovering late on the roll.

The best guards and wings don’t solve this with athleticism alone. They solve it with early positioning and a footwork pattern that can be taught. That is the development edge.

Development Notes for Coaches and Trainers

This is teachable earlier than most coaches assume.

  1. Isolate the two actions. Drill shrinking space and the swing step separately before combining them. Players who try both at once rush the first step and get caught on the screen.
  2. Teach the swing step as a hip-and-foot action. The common error is spinning the whole torso. The effective version keeps the chest relatively square while the lower body clears. Holiday and Wade are the best film for this because their upper-body control is so clean.
  3. Add a live handler early. Static screen work builds false confidence. Once the footwork is competent, put a moving ball handler in front of the defender, because the skill is shrinking space against movement, not a cone.
  4. Measure the result, not the form. Track how often the pocket pass is contested or denied, how often the handler is pushed into a tougher angle, and how fast the defender recovers to the roll man. Disruption matters more than how the footwork looks.
  5. Connect it to identity. Players who own this sequence start seeing themselves as problem-solvers, not just athletes. That shift carries into other parts of their game.

These four clips are not nostalgia or highlight-reel defense. They are a technical consensus from four of the best on-ball defenders of the last twenty years. The footwork matches because the problem matches.

For coaches working with developing players, that consensus is the curriculum.

Source: Mike Jagacki on X. The film breakdown is his; the development notes are mine.
