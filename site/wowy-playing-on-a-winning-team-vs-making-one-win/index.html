WOWY: The Difference Between Playing on a Winning Team and Making One Win

Career winning percentage tells us what happened to a player’s team. WOWY helps us understand how much of that outcome was actually connected to the player. In an industry that routinely confuses production, reputation and winning, that distinction matters.

By Keil Moore, Editor-in-Chief · November 2, 2026

Domantas Sabonis has spent his NBA career putting up numbers that suggest one thing and accumulating a win-loss record that suggests something else.

That’s where the conversation gets interesting.

Sabonis has been an All-Star, an elite rebounder, a consistent triple-double threat and one of the league’s more productive offensive bigs. Yet his career winning percentage doesn’t exactly reflect the profile of a player with that level of production.

So what are we supposed to make of that?

Is Sabonis not as impactful as his numbers suggest? Has he spent too much of his career in unfavorable situations? Or are we using the wrong measurements to evaluate what an individual player actually contributes to winning?

I would argue it’s a combination of the last two.

Basketball has an evaluation problem. We routinely use team accomplishments to establish individual value, then turn around and use individual statistics to explain why a player deserves credit for those same accomplishments. The problem is that neither approach consistently isolates the player’s actual contribution.

A player can accumulate wins because he is surrounded by an exceptional roster. Another can produce at an All-Star level while spending years on teams that struggle to compete.

Neither circumstance tells us enough about the individual.

This is where WOWY becomes an interesting part of the evaluation process.

What WOWY Actually Measures

WOWY stands for With Or Without You.

The concept is straightforward: compare how a team performs when a particular player is available versus how it performs when that player is absent.

We’re not talking about traditional on-court versus off-court statistics measured by minutes. We’re looking at the team’s performance across games with and without the player.

The question changes from:

How many games did his team win?

To:

What happened to his team when he wasn’t available?

That is a much more useful starting point when evaluating individual impact.

Thinking Basketball developed WOWYR — With Or Without You, Regressed — as a game-level adjusted plus-minus approach. It uses game results and regression to estimate player impact, allowing comparisons across different eras, including periods before modern play-by-play data existed. It is not simply a raw win-loss split with a different name.

That distinction matters because raw WOWY has limitations. A player’s absence can coincide with other injuries, changes in the rotation, differences in competition and entirely different circumstances.

Still, the underlying principle is valuable.

If a team consistently performs at a significantly lower level without a particular player, we have evidence that his presence matters. If the team maintains its performance without him, we have reason to investigate how much of its success is attributable to that player.

Neither conclusion should be automatic. But both are worth investigating.

The objective isn’t to determine whether a player has been on winning teams. It’s to determine whether he has meaningfully influenced the probability of those teams winning.

That is a different standard of evaluation.

Before We Use WOWY, Understand Its Limitations

There is a tendency in basketball analytics to find a useful statistic and immediately turn it into a definitive answer.

That’s a mistake.

WOWY is a diagnostic tool, not a complete player evaluation model. Three considerations should guide how we interpret it.

1. Sample size matters.

A player missing six games doesn’t provide enough information to establish a reliable pattern. Even 20 or 30 games can be misleading depending on the circumstances.

The more observations we have, the more useful the comparison becomes. But there is no universal number of games that automatically makes a WOWY split reliable.

2. Net rating tells us more than wins alone.

A team’s record is important, but it doesn’t always explain the quality of its performance.

Point differential and net rating provide additional context. A team that moves from +6.0 to -3.0 points per 100 possessions when a player is absent is experiencing a substantial performance change.

That is more informative than simply comparing two win percentages.

3. The surrounding personnel matter.

Who replaces the player? Are other starters missing? Is the team playing a different style? Are the minutes being redistributed to players who cannot replicate the same responsibilities?

These questions are essential.

This is why I would use WOWY to identify a potential impact pattern and adjusted metrics such as RAPM and EPM to investigate it further.

RAPM attempts to isolate a player’s contribution while accounting for teammates and opponents. EPM uses a different predictive modeling framework to estimate overall player impact.

No single metric should determine the evaluation.

The strongest conclusions come when multiple measurements point in the same direction.

Four Players. Four Different Evaluation Problems.

The best way to understand WOWY is to examine players whose career narratives look different when we move beyond traditional winning percentage.

Case 1Nikola Jokić: The System Is the Player

Jokić is one of the clearest examples of a player whose impact extends well beyond conventional production.

We already know what he does statistically. The scoring, rebounding and passing are extraordinary. What makes Jokić different is how his presence changes the responsibilities and effectiveness of everyone around him.

Denver’s offense is built around his ability to process the floor, manipulate defensive positioning, create advantages and make decisions before the defense has time to recover.

He is not simply a player who benefits from an offensive system.

He is the foundation of that system.

The 2025-26 numbers offer an interesting illustration. Denver posted a +6.5 net rating with Jokić and -0.2 without him, according to StatMuse’s team splits.

With Jokić+6.5Net rating · 65 games
Without Jokić−0.2Net rating · 17 games

2025-26 regular season. Source: StatMuse.

That is a 6.7-point difference per 100 possessions.

The more important question is why.

Without Jokić, Denver loses its primary offensive organizer. The team has to redistribute playmaking, change its offensive entry points and find different ways to create efficient possessions.

Replacing his scoring is one challenge. Replacing his decision-making is another.

This is where conventional player evaluation often falls short. We tend to measure what a player produces instead of examining what his teammates are able to produce because he is on the floor.

Jokić creates value through his own production, but also through the opportunities he creates for others.

That is the difference between being a high-level individual performer and being an offensive infrastructure.

WOWY takeawayJokić’s value isn’t limited to what appears in his box score. His impact is reflected in how much of Denver’s offensive identity depends on his availability.

Case 2Giannis Antetokounmpo: The Floor Raiser

Giannis presents a different evaluation challenge.

His value is often discussed through championships, MVP awards and Milwaukee’s extended run as one of the league’s more successful organizations.

But the more interesting question is what happens when the roster around him becomes less effective.

A player’s ability to elevate a good team is important. His ability to keep a declining team competitive is a different skill.

That distinction is particularly relevant when evaluating Giannis.

His combination of rim pressure, transition offense, physicality and defensive versatility gave Milwaukee a foundation that was difficult to replicate.

When he was unavailable, the Bucks didn’t simply lose their leading scorer. They lost a player who forced opposing defenses to adjust their personnel, coverage and help responsibilities.

That created consequences throughout the lineup.

His final season in Milwaukee provides a useful case study. In 2025-26, the Bucks went 17-19 with Giannis and 15-31 without him.

With Giannis17-19.472 · 36 games
Without Giannis15-31.326 · 46 games

2025-26 regular season (Bucks finished 32-50). Sources: StatMuse, 2025-26 Bucks season.

The raw comparison requires context, particularly given the team’s injuries and changing roster circumstances. But the larger evaluation question remains relevant.

How much of Milwaukee’s competitive ability was directly connected to Giannis?

I would argue that his greatest value is not simply that he made Milwaukee better. It’s that he gave the organization a competitive foundation even when the supporting pieces were not operating at an optimal level.

That is floor-raising.

There is a meaningful difference between a player who helps a good team become great and one who prevents an average team from becoming bad.

The best players can do both.

Milwaukee traded him to Miami in June, which makes 2026-27 a WOWY experiment of its own: one franchise learning to play without him, another learning what it looks like with him.

WOWY takeawayGiannis demonstrates why player impact cannot be evaluated exclusively through team success. Sometimes the most valuable contribution is preventing a roster from falling further than it otherwise would.

Case 3Domantas Sabonis: Production Versus Impact

Sabonis is the most interesting evaluation exercise in this discussion because his production creates a legitimate debate about his overall value.

His offensive numbers are difficult to dismiss.

He rebounds at an elite level, facilitates offense from the frontcourt and provides a level of consistent production that most teams would welcome.

But there is a difference between producing at an All-Star level and influencing winning at an All-NBA level.

That is where the evaluation becomes more complicated.

Sacramento’s 2022-23 season demonstrated what a productive Sabonis-led offense could accomplish. The Kings went from a lengthy playoff drought to 48 wins, with Sabonis playing a central role in their offensive identity.

He provided structure.

His screening, passing, rebounding and ability to operate as a hub helped create opportunities for the perimeter players around him.

But the question is whether that offensive value translates into the same level of impact when the game changes.

The postseason introduces a different evaluation environment. Opponents have more time to prepare, defensive matchups become more deliberate and weaknesses are targeted repeatedly.

Sabonis’ defensive limitations become more relevant in those situations.

This is an important distinction for player evaluation: offensive production can raise a team’s floor, but defensive limitations can restrict its ceiling.

We should not confuse those two outcomes.

His 2025-26 Sacramento splits also illustrate why raw WOWY can be misleading. The Kings went 3-16 in the 19 games Sabonis played and 19-44 in the 63 he missed.

With Sabonis3-16.158 · 19 games
Without Sabonis19-44.302 · 63 games

2025-26 regular season (Kings finished 22-60). Source: StatMuse.

Taken literally, that comparison would suggest the team performed better without him. But a team in transition, dealing with injuries or operating under different competitive objectives cannot be evaluated through a simple availability split. Sabonis spent part of that season trying to play through a torn meniscus before season-ending surgery in February.

The correct response is not to ignore the numbers. It is to investigate them.

What was the roster? Who was available? What changed tactically? What was the team’s competitive situation?

That is what serious evaluation requires.

I would rather have a conversation about Sabonis’ offensive value, defensive limitations and portability than reduce his entire career to a win percentage.

WOWY takeawaySabonis is a reminder that production, team impact and championship-level portability are three different evaluation questions. A player can be excellent in one area without being equally dominant in all three.

Case 4Mario Chalmers: The Benefit of a Winning Environment

Mario Chalmers provides a useful contrast.

His career winning percentage was heavily influenced by his time in Miami, where he played alongside LeBron James, Dwyane Wade and Chris Bosh.

Chalmers was a capable NBA guard. He could defend, make open shots, handle the ball and operate within an established offensive structure.

Those skills mattered.

But his career record also reflects the advantages of playing in an exceptional basketball environment.

Miami was built to win championships. The organization had elite talent, an established identity and a coaching staff that understood how to maximize its personnel.

Chalmers benefited from that environment.

That does not diminish his contribution. It simply places it in the appropriate context.

Consider what happens when a player moves from a championship-caliber roster to a team operating under entirely different circumstances.

His win percentage can change dramatically without his individual ability changing at the same rate.

That is why career winning percentage should never be treated as a standalone measure of individual impact.

The same principle applies in the opposite direction.

A player who spends his career on losing teams should not automatically be classified as someone who cannot win.

The evaluation must account for what the player actually contributes.

Chalmers is not proof that every role player on a championship team is a passenger. He is an example of why team success cannot be assigned equally to every player on the roster.

WOWY takeawayWinning environments matter. The challenge is separating the value a player creates from the advantages he inherits.

What the Historical Examples Tell Us

Bill Walton is one of the most compelling historical examples of the value of this type of analysis.

Thinking Basketball’s WOWY research identified Walton as having an extraordinary impact on Portland, with his presence improving the Blazers by more than eight points per game — the best WOWY score in NBA history in that analysis.

That is significant because Walton’s traditional scoring averages do not fully communicate his impact.

His ability to rebound, protect the rim, facilitate offense and influence the tempo of a game changed how Portland functioned.

The larger lesson is that individual value cannot be reduced to scoring volume.

Some players influence the game by creating opportunities. Others eliminate opportunities for opponents. Some control the pace, organize the offense or provide defensive structure.

Those contributions do not always receive the same attention as points per game.

But they are often the difference between a team that looks competitive and one that actually wins.

The Evaluation Framework We Should Be Using

The important word is complementary.

These measurements are not interchangeable, and they should not be treated as if they answer the same question.

A player can rank highly in production and only moderately in adjusted impact. Another can have modest box-score numbers while consistently influencing the quality of a team’s possessions.

That is why evaluation requires more than sorting a spreadsheet.

It requires understanding basketball.

Jokić is an example of elite production and elite impact working together. Giannis demonstrates the value of a player who can provide a competitive foundation. Sabonis illustrates the difference between offensive production and complete two-way impact. Chalmers reminds us that team success is not automatically individual achievement.

The point is not to establish a new ranking system.

It is to improve the questions we ask.

Why This Matters Beyond the NBA

This conversation has implications well beyond professional basketball.

In grassroots basketball, recruiting and player development, we routinely make the same evaluation mistakes.

  • We look at a player’s record without examining the roster.
  • We evaluate scoring without understanding efficiency or usage.
  • We reward players for being part of successful programs without determining how much they contributed to that success.
  • We also overlook players whose production is limited by their role, supporting cast or developmental environment.

A player’s value is not always reflected in the team’s record. And a team’s record is not always a reliable indicator of the value of every player on its roster.

For coaches, this should influence how we assess personnel.

For scouts, it should influence how we project players into different environments.

For recruiting departments, it should influence how we evaluate portability.

Can the player contribute when the system changes? Can he make teammates better? Can he impact the game without needing the ball? Does his value translate against better competition?

Those are more meaningful questions than simply asking how many games his current team wins.

A player who can make a struggling team more competitive possesses a transferable skill. A player whose production depends entirely on a particular roster or system may have a different kind of value.

Neither profile is inherently bad. But they should not be evaluated the same way.

The Bottom Line

Winning matters. It always will.

But the basketball industry has to become more disciplined about how it assigns credit for winning.

Career winning percentage tells us what happened during a player’s time with a franchise. WOWY gives us another way to examine what changed when that player was unavailable.

Neither tells the entire story.

The best evaluations combine production, team context, adjusted impact, film and an understanding of how a player’s skill set translates across different environments.

That is the standard.

We have spent too much time debating who wins and not enough time examining why they win, what they contribute and whether those contributions can be replicated somewhere else.

The next evolution of player evaluation isn’t finding another statistic to put on a graphic. It’s getting better at understanding what the statistics are actually telling us.

And perhaps the more important question isn’t whether a player has been part of winning.

It’s whether winning becomes more difficult when he isn’t there.

That’s the conversation worth having.
