Recruiting Reality NIL and revenue sharing

The Recruiting Question Families Aren’t Asking

Does your school actually pay athletes? Revenue sharing has changed college basketball. Before families get caught up in the promise of getting paid, they need to know who is paying, how much is moving, and whether their school is even participating.

By Keil Moore · October 30, 2026

Disclosure: HoopThink is affiliated with The Westview Group, which also does athlete-representation and NIL-related work.

College basketball recruiting has a new selling point: money.

Not playing time. Not development. Not a coaching staff’s ability to prepare a young woman for the next level. Money.

Financial opportunity is a legitimate part of the recruiting conversation. But there is one detail families should investigate before celebrating a potential payday:

Does the school actually pay its athletes?

I know. Not exactly the kind of question that gets a highlight reel going. But it may be one of the most important questions a family asks, because in the new college athletics economy, participating in revenue sharing and actually distributing money are two very different things.

Everybody Is Talking About Revenue Sharing. Let’s Talk About the Revenue.

The House settlement changed the financial landscape of college athletics. Participating Division I schools can now compensate athletes directly within an established framework.

For 2025-26, the first year of the new structure, court filings by the College Sports Commission showed 319 Division I schools opted in. Of those, 307 made direct payments, totaling about $1.77 billion to 34,915 athletes across 45 sports, as ESPN reported on October 1, 2026. Some outlets round the total to $1.8 billion.

The money is moving. This is no longer a theoretical debate.

But here is where families need to slow down. A school opting into the settlement does not mean every athlete on its roster receives a check. It does not mean every sport has an equivalent budget. And it does not mean a coach who mentions a financial opportunity has the budget, the authority or the intention to deliver what a family thinks it heard.

The spending numbers show the gap. ESPN reported that 68 schools reached or came within 5% of the cap. ESPN does not report how many opted-in schools paid nothing, but the arithmetic is simple: 319 opted in and 307 made payments, which leaves 12.

Read that again.

A school can opt into revenue sharing and still pay an athlete nothing directly.

That distinction belongs in every serious recruiting conversation.

The $20.5 Million Headline Can Be Misleading

The settlement’s first-year framework set a revenue-sharing cap of about $20.5 million per participating school. It is an easy talking point and an easy number to misunderstand.

The cap is not a guaranteed budget for every athletic department, much less a guaranteed payment to every athlete. Actual distributions vary by institution, sport, roster priorities and individual circumstances. And the filings reported by ESPN do not break out basketball.

A family evaluating a women’s basketball program should not assume that a school’s overall financial capacity becomes a meaningful allocation for women’s basketball, or that an athletic department’s decision to participate means its roster will receive equal amounts.

The relevant question is not whether the institution has money. It is how the institution intends to allocate it, and what that means for your daughter.

Ask the staff to explain the program’s actual compensation structure. Is there an institutional allocation? How is it determined? What portion is guaranteed, if any? Is the figure being discussed an approved offer, an estimate, or an aspiration?

There is a difference between a financial plan and a recruiting pitch. Families deserve to know which one they are hearing.

Opting Out Is a Real Strategy, Too

Not every Division I school has chosen to participate. The Ivy League opted out, and the Patriot League opted out of revenue sharing. Opt-in status can change from year to year, so confirm any school’s current position.

For some schools, opting out reflects institutional priorities, financial reality or other constraints. Whatever the reason, the recruiting implications are real. An opt-out school does not offer institutional revenue sharing through this framework. It may still offer scholarships, need-based aid where applicable and chances to earn third-party NIL income. Those matter. They are not interchangeable with a direct institutional payment.

A school cannot offer revenue sharing through a structure it has declined to enter. A family should not discover that after committing.

NIL Is Not the Same as Revenue Sharing

Families hear about NIL deals, collectives, brand partnerships, institutional payments and revenue sharing as if they were different names for the same pot of money. They are not.

Institutional revenue sharing is direct compensation through a participating school’s framework. Third-party NIL compensation comes from outside parties who pay for the use of an athlete’s name, image and likeness. Those deals can be legitimate opportunities. They can also vary widely in value, reliability and the work required to earn the money.

Division I athletes must report third-party NIL deals worth $600 or more in the aggregate through NIL Go, the College Sports Commission’s platform, which was built with assistance from Deloitte. That means a player can pursue qualifying NIL opportunities even at a school that is not paying her directly. But the existence of those opportunities does not mean the school is paying her.

If a coach says, “There is money available here,” the follow-up is simple: What kind of money? From whom? Under what terms? That is not being difficult. It is being financially literate.

The Roster Question Nobody Should Ignore

The money also affects roster construction. Participating schools face a 15-player roster limit in both men’s and women’s basketball, while schools that did not opt in may keep the older scholarship-limit model.

Neither choice automatically makes a program better. The question is which structure creates the strongest opportunity for a particular athlete. Evaluate the entire offer: the likelihood of playing, the quality of development, the academic fit, the coaching staff, the roster outlook, the scholarship package and the actual financial terms.

A bigger roster is not inherently better. A revenue-sharing check is not inherently better than a strong development environment. A promise of future money should never substitute for clarity about what is on the table today.

Five Questions Every Family Should Ask

  1. Has the school opted into the House settlement for the upcoming academic year? Confirm its current status with the compliance office, not just in a recruiting conversation.
  2. Does the program make direct institutional payments? An opt-in designation alone does not answer this.
  3. What compensation is actually being offered to my child? Ask for the amount, timing and conditions, and whether it is guaranteed or contingent.
  4. Are third-party NIL opportunities being presented as if they were school payments? Identify the payer, the deliverables, the terms and the reporting requirements.
  5. How does the financial opportunity fit into the complete package? Weigh the scholarship, playing opportunity, development, academics, roster situation and long-term pathway alongside compensation.

These questions are not only for high-major recruiting. They matter at every level where families are weighing the costs and commitments of a college opportunity.

The Bottom Line: Verify the Offer, Not the Sales Pitch

College athletics is operating in a new financial environment, and families need to adjust how they evaluate opportunities. The old questions still matter: scholarship, playing time, development. Now families must also understand a school’s compensation structure and tell the difference between money that exists in theory and money actually being offered to an athlete.

Institutional participation is widespread. Payment practices vary substantially. Some participating schools have reported no direct payments. That makes school-level verification essential. Opt-in decisions can change from year to year. Verify the current position with the school’s compliance office and the College Sports Commission’s reporting.

The goal is not to win the recruiting conversation. It is to make the right decision. If a program is selling a financial opportunity, asking how it works should not make anyone uncomfortable. Being able to explain it clearly should be a basic expectation.

Recruiting is about finding the right fit: the basketball fit, the academic fit, the developmental fit and the financial fit. The money matters. So does knowing where it is coming from.

What should families prioritize when evaluating a college basketball offer: guaranteed compensation, player development, or the complete package?
