Recruiting Guide NIL for families

NIL, Explained for Families

NIL is not one thing. It is several kinds of money with different sources, different rules and different paperwork. Five terms, two buckets and four questions to ask on every official visit.

By Keil Moore · November 12, 2026

Disclosure: HoopThink is affiliated with The Westview Group, which also does athlete-representation and NIL-related work.

If your family is in the middle of recruiting, you have heard a lot of NIL numbers. Some sound huge. Some sound vague. Most sound confusing.

That confusion is normal, because NIL is not one thing. It is several kinds of money with different sources, different rules and different paperwork. Learn the five terms below and you can walk into any visit and ask sharper questions.

This is general education, not legal advice. When a school makes a specific promise, get it in writing and confirm it with that school’s compliance office.

Start With the Two Buckets

Every dollar falls into one of two buckets. The bucket decides what counts toward the school’s cap, what gets reported and what a coach can actually promise.

1. Money from the school. Direct payments from the school itself. It counts toward the school’s cap, and the school manages it.

2. Money from outside. Brands, collectives and businesses. It does not come from the school. Outside deals of $600 or more go through NIL Go, the clearinghouse that checks for a valid business purpose and fair market value.

An athlete can have both. Revenue sharing plus outside NIL is allowed. So the question is never just "how much?" It is "who is paying, for what work, and is it in writing?"

1. NIL Deals

What it is: The umbrella term for all of this. An athlete is paid for the use of her name, image and likeness, not for playing.

What it looks like: Real work: a post, an ad, an appearance, an autograph session, a camp.

What it means for your family: If there is no work attached, be skeptical. A real NIL deal spells out exactly what your athlete has to do.

Ask: What exact deliverables are required? When are they due? Who owns the content once it is posted?

2. Collectives

What it is: A donor-funded group tied to one school. Fans and boosters pool their money, and the collective sets up NIL deals for athletes at that school.

What it looks like: Payment for appearances, social posts, autographs or community events tied to that school’s fan base.

What it means for your family: A collective is outside money, not school money. Deals of $600 or more must be reported to NIL Go and pass a fair-market-value check.

Red flag: A collective deal cannot be pay-for-play, meaning payment just for enrolling or staying. If an offer sounds like "come here and you get this," treat it as a warning, not a selling point.

Ask: What work is tied to this deal? Has it been submitted to NIL Go? What happens if it is not cleared?

3. Brand Partnerships

What it is: A direct deal with a real business. The business pays from its marketing budget because it wants your athlete’s reach, not because it wants her at a certain school.

What it looks like: A local restaurant, a training brand, a clothing company or a national brand paying for posts, ads or appearances.

What it means for your family: This is the cleanest form of NIL and the easiest to explain to compliance. The value comes from the athlete’s audience and work, not from a school decision.

Ask: Is pay tied to followers, appearances or sales? Does an exclusivity clause block other deals? How long does the deal last, and does it travel if your athlete transfers?

4. Institutional Payments

What it is: Any money that comes directly from the school.

What it looks like: Most often revenue sharing. It can also include other direct payments the school is permitted to make under current rules.

What it means for your family: These payments count toward the school’s cap, and the school manages them. They are different from third-party NIL, which is everything else.

Do not let a coach blend the two buckets in conversation. Ask which bucket each dollar is in.

Ask: Is this in the offer letter? Is it guaranteed year to year, or reviewed each season? What happens if there is a coaching change or an injury?

5. Revenue Sharing

What it is: The main type of institutional payment, created by the House settlement. Schools that opt in can share athletics revenue directly with athletes.

What it looks like: For 2026-27 the cap is about $21.3 million per school, across all sports. That is a school-wide pool, not a per-player promise.

What it means for your family: Nothing here is guaranteed per athlete. Each school decides how to split the pool between sports and players, so basketball may get a very different slice than football, and one school’s split can look nothing like another’s. Two schools can quote the same $21.3 million pool and offer your family two very different realities.

Ask: How does this school split its pool? How much is Year 1 versus Years 2 through 4? Is the amount in writing, or is it a projection?

Ask This on Every Visit

You do not need to memorize every rule. You need a checklist that forces clarity.

  • Who is paying? The school, a collective or a brand?
  • What work is required? Posts, appearances, camps or something else?
  • Is it in writing? Verbal promises do not count.
  • Does it go through NIL Go? An outside deal of $600 or more should.

If a school cannot answer those four questions clearly, that tells you something too.

Save this article and send it to another family going through recruiting. Families who understand the terms make better decisions, and they ask better questions when it matters most.

NIL Glossary for Families

Keep this handy during visits. If a coach, collective or brand uses a term you do not recognize, check it here.

  • Boosters: Fans, alumni or donors who support a school’s athletics program financially. Many collectives are funded by boosters.
  • Brand Partnership: A direct deal with a real business that pays from its marketing budget for your athlete’s reach and work, such as posts, ads or appearances.
  • Cap: The maximum a school can share directly with athletes in a year through revenue sharing. For 2026-27 it is about $21.3 million per school across all sports.
  • Collective: A donor-funded group tied to one school. It pools money from fans and boosters, then sets up NIL deals for that school’s athletes.
  • Compliance: The school office that checks whether deals, payments and recruiting activity follow the rules. When in doubt, ask compliance to confirm in writing.
  • Deliverables: The specific work a deal requires, such as a number of posts, an appearance, an autograph session or a camp.
  • Exclusivity: A clause that blocks your athlete from working with competing brands. Ask how long it lasts and which categories it covers.
  • Fair Market Value: What a business would reasonably pay a similar athlete for similar work. Outside deals of $600 or more must pass this check through NIL Go.
  • House Settlement: The settlement that created the current revenue-sharing system, allowing schools that opt in to share athletics revenue directly with athletes.
  • Institutional Payments: Any money that comes directly from the school. It counts toward the school’s cap and is managed by the school.
  • NIL: Name, image and likeness. An athlete can be paid for the use of her identity in marketing and promotions.
  • NIL Go: The clearinghouse that reviews outside NIL deals of $600 or more for a valid business purpose and fair market value.
  • Offer Letter: The school’s written document outlining scholarship, revenue sharing and other school-provided terms. Verbal promises do not replace it.
  • Pay-for-Play: Payment simply for playing, enrolling or staying at a school. It is not allowed as an NIL deal. Real NIL is tied to real work and real business value.
  • Revenue Sharing: The main type of institutional payment. Schools that opt in share a portion of athletics revenue directly with athletes, and each school decides how to split its pool between sports and players.
  • Third-Party NIL: Any NIL money that does not come from the school. Brand partnerships and collective deals are both third-party NIL.
