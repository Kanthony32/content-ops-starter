NIL, Explained: 5 Terms Every Recruit, Parent, and Coach Should Know Before the Next Visit

Recruiting has become a money conversation, and the vocabulary is working against the people who need it most. Five terms, two buckets and four questions to ask on every visit.

By Keil Moore, Editor-in-Chief, HoopThink · November 12, 2026

Disclosure: HoopThink is affiliated with The Westview Group, which also does athlete-representation and NIL-related work.

Recruiting has become a money conversation, and the vocabulary is working against the people who need it most. Numbers get thrown around as if they mean the same thing. Some sound enormous. Some sound vague. Very few come with paperwork attached.

On HoopThink, we treat recruiting money the way we treat a box score: What is the number? Who produced it? Can you verify it? NIL is not one revenue stream. It is a few different kinds of money, with different rules, different sources and different compliance paths. Learn the five terms below and you will walk into any visit with a scouting report on the pitch, not just a feeling about it.

This is general education, not legal advice. When a school makes a specific promise, get it in writing and confirm it with that school’s compliance office.

Start With the Two Buckets

Every dollar lands in one of two places, and that split decides what counts toward a cap, what has to be reported and what can actually be promised.

  • Bucket 1: School money. Direct payments from the institution. It counts toward the school cap, and the school manages it.
  • Bucket 2: Outside money. Brands, collectives and businesses. It runs through NIL Go, the clearinghouse that checks for a valid business purpose and fair market value.

An athlete can draw from both. Revenue sharing plus outside NIL is allowed. The question is never just "how much." It is: who is paying, for what work, and is it in writing?

1. NIL Deals

What it is: The umbrella term. Compensation for the use of name, image and likeness, not for playing.

What it looks like: Real work. A post, an ad, an appearance, an autograph session, a camp.

Read it like a scout: No work attached means no deal. A legitimate agreement spells out exactly what the athlete has to produce.

Ask: What are the exact deliverables? When are they due? Who owns the content once it is posted?

2. Collectives

What it is: A donor-funded group tied to one school. Boosters pool money, and the collective sets up NIL deals for athletes at that school.

What it looks like: Payment for appearances, social posts, autographs or community events tied to that school’s fan base.

What it means for your family: Collective money is outside money, not school money. Deals of $600 or more must be reported to NIL Go and pass a fair-market-value check.

Red flag: Pay-for-play is not allowed. Compensation for enrolling or staying is not an NIL deal. If the pitch sounds like "come here and you get this," you are hearing a warning, not a selling point.

Ask: What work is tied to this deal? Has it been submitted to NIL Go? What happens if it does not clear?

3. Brand Partnerships

What it is: A direct deal with a real business. The business pays from its marketing budget because it wants the athlete’s reach, not because it wants the athlete at a particular school.

What it looks like: A local restaurant, a training brand, an apparel company or a national name paying for posts, ads or appearances.

What it means for your family: This is the cleanest form of NIL and the easiest to defend to compliance. The value comes from the athlete’s audience and work, not from a roster decision.

Ask: Is pay tied to followers, appearances or sales? Does exclusivity block other deals? How long does the deal run, and does it follow the athlete through a transfer?

4. Institutional Payments

What it is: Any money that comes directly from the school.

What it looks like: Mostly revenue sharing, plus any other direct payments the school is permitted to make under current rules.

What it means for your family: It counts toward the school’s cap, and the school manages it. Everything else is third-party NIL.

Do not let a coach blend the two buckets together in conversation. Make them tell you which bucket each dollar sits in.

Ask: Is this in the offer letter? Is it guaranteed year to year, or reviewed each season? What happens with a coaching change or an injury?

5. Revenue Sharing

What it is: The main institutional payment, created by the House settlement. Schools that opt in can share athletics revenue directly with athletes.

What it looks like: For 2026-27, the cap is about $21.3 million per school, across all sports.

The analysis: That number is a school-wide pool, not a per-player promise. Schools decide how to divide it between sports and players. Basketball’s slice can look nothing like football’s, and one school’s split can look nothing like another’s. Two programs can quote the same $21.3 million and offer two very different realities.

Ask: How does this program split its pool? What does Year 1 look like compared with Years 2 through 4? Is the number in writing, or is it a projection?

The Reform Question

Here is where I stand. The burden of decoding this system has landed on the families least equipped to carry it. A recruit and a parent should not need a glossary to tell whether an offer is real. Clear offer letters, consistent disclosure of how each school splits its pool, and public visibility into NIL Go outcomes would do more for recruiting integrity than any amount of sideline commentary.

The other side deserves a fair hearing. Supporters of the current framework argue that a defined cap and a central clearinghouse give schools and athletes one common rulebook, which beats the patchwork of state laws and booster-driven deals that came before it. Critics respond that a cap without a standard disclosure format still leaves families reading offers alone. The debate is far from settled.

Ask This on Every Visit

You do not need to memorize every rule. You need a checklist that forces clarity.

  • Who is paying? The school, a collective or a brand?
  • What work is required? Posts, appearances, camps or something else?
  • Is it in writing? Verbal promises do not count.
  • Does it go through NIL Go? If it is an outside deal of $600 or more, it should.

If a program cannot answer those four questions clearly, that is the data point you needed.

Question for the room: Which of these four would your program fail today?

Forward this to a family in the middle of recruiting. The families who understand the terms make better decisions, and they ask sharper questions when it matters most.

NIL Glossary: Field Reference

Keep this part handy on visits. If a coach, collective or brand uses a term you do not recognize, check here first.

  • Boosters: Fans, alumni or donors who financially support a school’s athletics program. Many collectives are booster-funded.
  • Brand Partnership: A direct deal with a real business that pays from its marketing budget for the athlete’s reach and work, such as posts, ads or appearances.
  • Cap: The maximum a school can share directly with athletes in a given year through revenue sharing. For 2026-27, about $21.3 million per school across all sports.
  • Collective: A donor-funded group tied to one school. It pools fan and booster money and sets up NIL deals for that school’s athletes.
  • Compliance: The school office that checks whether deals, payments and recruiting activity follow the rules. When in doubt, have compliance confirm in writing.
  • Deliverables: The specific work a deal requires, such as a number of posts, an appearance, an autograph session or a camp.
  • Exclusivity: A clause that blocks the athlete from working with competing brands. Ask how long it lasts and which categories it covers.
  • Fair Market Value: What a business would reasonably pay a similar athlete for similar work. Outside deals of $600 or more must pass this check through NIL Go.
  • House Settlement: The settlement that created the current revenue-sharing system. It allows schools that opt in to share athletics revenue directly with athletes.
  • Institutional Payments: Any money that comes directly from the school. It counts toward the cap, and the school manages it.
  • NIL: Name, Image and Likeness. It means an athlete can be paid for the use of that identity in marketing and promotions.
  • NIL Go: The clearinghouse that reviews outside NIL deals of $600 or more for a valid business purpose and fair market value.
  • Offer Letter: The written school document outlining scholarship, revenue sharing and other school-provided terms. Verbal promises do not replace it.
  • Pay-for-Play: Payment for playing, enrolling or staying at a school. It is not allowed as an NIL deal. Real NIL is tied to real work and real business value.
  • Revenue Sharing: The main institutional payment. Schools that opt in share a portion of athletics revenue directly with athletes, and each school decides how to split its pool between sports and players.
  • Third-Party NIL: Any NIL money that does not come from the school. Brand partnerships and collective deals are both third-party NIL.
